# Environments experience: ideas and hypotheses backlog

The durable place where research turns into product ideas for the LayerLens Environments experience. Unlike a weekly brief (ephemeral) or a competitive entry (about someone else), an idea here **accumulates evidence over time** and moves up a maturity ladder until Javier decides what to build.

The system proposes and gathers evidence. It never builds, files tickets, or decides. Javier decides, and promotes ready ideas to Linear/Figma himself.

Ideas with a product-shaped hypothesis also get a row in `features/matrix.csv` (F-xxxx), the ranked sheet where Impact, Effort, evidence-derived Confidence, and Priority live. This file is the deep-traceability layer behind that sheet.

## Maturity ladder

- **L0 Nascent**: one source, or a bare idea. Interesting, not actionable.
- **L1 Developing**: multiple independent sources, OR one strong source plus a clear user outcome and a validation plan.
- **L2 Ready to spec**: a real problem, a named persona, a user outcome stated as "better for whom, by how much," at least one open design question it answers, and a concrete way to validate. This is where Javier promotes it out of the repo.
- **L3 Decided**: build / park / drop, with Javier's reason and date.

Rule: **a single mention never reaches L2.** One paper is a lead, not a mandate.

## How an idea earns its place (schema)

Each entry records: the problem/pain point, the persona, the user outcome (time to diagnose, comprehension, correction, recovery, reliability, effort, cost), the linked open design question(s) from `LAYERLENS_CONTEXT.md`, accumulating evidence (claim IDs + links), a testable hypothesis, how to validate, and maturity + last update.

## I-0001: Reliability-aware run counts and a pass^k readout

- **Problem / pain point**: A single pass or accuracy number hides whether an agent behaves the same way twice. A user can ship an agent that "passed" once and then fails unpredictably in production.
- **Persona**: Evan (evaluation owner), Taylor (accountable lead, shares the readiness report).
- **User outcome**: users can separate capability (can it ever succeed) from reliability (does it succeed every time), and spend runs where reliability matters instead of running everything once or everything many times.
- **Open design question(s)**: #5 (reliability versus one lucky pass), with #1/#9 (comparison).
- **Evidence** (accumulating):
  - C-0008, "Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability" (Princeton, ICML 2026): pass@k vs pass^k, 12 reliability metrics across 4 dimensions, finding minimal reliability gains despite capability gains. https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16666
  - C-0013, "Same Winners, Different Success Rates": ordinal/set-agreement benchmarks provably hide reliability. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34215
  - C-0019, "Identical Runs, Different Results": 584 real coding-agent runs — run-to-run variance (median 0.0107 AUC) exceeds agent-to-agent difference (mean 0.0095 AUC); three-run comparisons rank pairings unreliably; a third, independent, real-world evidence family. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33812
  - C-0053, ThinkingBox (incomplete access, dates/numbers disputed across sources): reportedly a ~40-point gap between pass@1 (65.36%) and pass^20 (25.25%) on a 507-task stateful business-workflow benchmark — a vivid number if confirmed, pending a primary-source check. (not yet confirmed)
  - C-0070, "Equivalence testing and run-count design" (directly read abstract, 2026-10-26): a TOST-based protocol plus a closed-form formula converting an equivalence margin and target power into runs-per-method — the first source giving this idea a principled mechanism for "how many runs k," not just evidence that too few mislead. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23700v1
  - C-0087, "Beyond pass@1: A Reliability Science Framework for Long-Horizon LLM Agents" (incomplete access, 2026-10-26): 23,392 episodes across 396 tasks find reliability decays specifically as task *duration* grows, not only across repeated runs — a new dimension for when extra run-count scrutiny matters most. https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29231
- **Hypothesis** (Javier's, refined): LayerLens holds the task, the grader type, and, because minting is deterministic and free, can cheaply observe run-to-run variance. So it can (a) surface **pass^k as a first-class reliability readout** beside accuracy, and (b) **recommend how many attempts k** a task warrants, single-shot where an answer-key grader on a stable task suffices, multiple where variance is detected or reliability is the question. The adaptive form ("reliability not yet established, run N more" vs "stable across k, stop") is stronger than a static task-type lookup, because variance is empirical.
- **Build caution (new, 2026-09-29)**: C-0024 ("Beyond Pass@k") shows some benchmarks compute pass@k wrong by conflating unit-test sub-results with independent rollouts, inflating scores by up to ~0.9 absolute in an illustrative case. Whatever "k attempts" LayerLens counts for a pass^k readout must be genuinely independent full-task rollouts, not sub-task or per-tool-call checks. https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14711 (via companion repo, github.com/nv78/Research-CodeBench)
- **Differentiator angle**: re-running is near-free here (deterministic free minting), so k-repeat reliability testing is economically viable in a way a paid or stateful competitor setup may not be. This makes the feature moat-aligned, not generic.
- **How to validate**: (1) the design exercise, sketch the readout (2026-W40 brief); (2) product check, do Runs/Insights show reliability by default today (open Q#5); (3) user test, show two runs with equal accuracy but different pass^k and see whether users pick the reliable one and can explain why.
- **Maturity**: L1 developing — now backed by five confirmed independent evidence families (including a principled run-count formula, C-0070) plus a clear outcome and validation plan, plus one pending (ThinkingBox, unconfirmed). **Evidence-complete**: the only thing standing between this and L2 is item (2), the product check, which needs someone with LayerLens product access; this idea should not need further external corroboration to be spec-ready once that check runs.
- **Last update**: 2026-10-26.

## I-0002: "Why is this not solvable" feasibility explanation at task authoring

- **Problem / pain point**: authors cannot easily tell whether a task is solvable, or why not; separately, agents barrel ahead on infeasible tasks and waste budget.
- **Persona**: Devon (agent engineer), Evan (evaluation owner).
- **User outcome**: authors design solvable scenarios faster and understand infeasibility at design time; evaluations stop wasting budget on impossible tasks.
- **Open design question(s)**: #4 (task feasibility).
- **Evidence**:
  - C-0007, FeasiGen (Edinburgh): masking the critical tools a task needs makes it infeasible; nine models detect infeasibility poorly (false-continue up to 73.9%); multi-agent planner-executor best pair 2.6%. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28532
  - C-0114, EnvOS (abstract read directly, 2026-11-09): a second independent source and the closest match yet — before any agent runs, EnvOS checks that the task remains solvable (alongside checking that a scripted change fires correctly and that the grader discriminates), and stress-tests the grading itself (on 26 blind-corrupted episodes it accepted none as clean; on 39 externally-generated ones it caught 25 vs. 4 for simple log checks). https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40246v1
- **Hypothesis**: LayerLens knows the environment's tools and the answer key, so it could compute and show whether a task's required tools and records are present, and explain "not solvable because tool X or record Y is missing," which is FeasiGen's critical-tool masking run in reverse.
- **How to validate**: prototype a feasibility check on an existing environment/task; author user test.
- **Maturity**: **L1 developing (promoted 2026-11-09** from L0): EnvOS is the second independent source this idea needed, and the first to actually ship a feasibility-check-before-run mechanism (not just measure the absence of one) alongside a grader-integrity stress-test. To reach L2: a named user outcome plus a validation plan, ideally confirmed with an author.
- **Last update**: 2026-11-09.

## I-0003: Attribution as a location, and every attribution a door

- **Problem / pain point**: users cannot tell whether a failure is the agent's, the environment's, the grader's, or infrastructure. A number alone does not say, and benchmark scores are frequently inflated by exploiting weak isolation.
- **Persona**: all (Devon, Evan, Riley especially).
- **User outcome**: faster and correct diagnosis; verdicts that can be trusted and defended.
- **Open design question(s)**: #2 (failure attribution). Aligns with the v3 direction's "failures light up in place."
- **Evidence**:
  - C-0001, BenchJack: 8/8 audited benchmarks exploitable via weak agent/evaluator isolation and accessible ground truth. https://github.com/benchjack/benchjack
  - C-0006, HackDetect: independent audit, 2,385 traces across 15 benchmarks, 67% and 66.7% exploit rates. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22368
  - C-0021, "Maintaining Benchmarks Against Increasingly Capable Agents": distinguishes evidenced reward-hacking from verifier weakness (env-vs-agent attribution, formalized); benchmark integrity is ongoing maintenance, not one-time. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34262
  - C-0022, "Failure-Transparent Agents": a distinct attribution category (unsupported post-failure success claims); a structured evidence contract cut false-success from 22.8% to 0.8%, independently validating LayerLens's existing "claims stored beside verdicts, never used as evidence" design. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35732
  - C-0039, BenchShield (incomplete access): instruments evaluation infrastructure prospectively to reconstruct the "reward-relevant trajectory" (how authority/information actually moved), rather than auditing after the fact — a structurally different, closer-to-LayerLens's-own-approach confirmation of the same attribution need; 456 human-adjudicated trajectories from 31,000+ public runs. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11028
  - C-0042, a 150-production-incident taxonomy (23 failure modes, 5 component-boundary categories) with measured resilience patterns (circuit breakers cut cascade propagation 89%; 3+ patterns cut MTTR 71%) — the first family grounded in real production incidents rather than benchmark/trace audits. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02503v1
  - C-0043, a real enterprise deployment: 92.6% of trials passing every final-output check still had an undetected trajectory deviation; dependency attribution cut 6.34 failed checks/run to 2.65 root causes — the most concrete "why trajectory-level attribution matters" number yet, from production rather than a benchmark. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01833v1
  - C-0046, "Discriminating Fixture Coverage": a verification suite can pass the standard "kills known-bad, passes known-good" bar and still certify a mutant and miss real defect classes — a caution directly applicable to LayerLens's own graders, not just the agents they grade. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02928v1
  - C-0048, Agent-Warden (abstract read directly): a kernel-level eBPF provenance monitor reconstructed a cross-process causal chain absent from the application-layer trace entirely — a literal illustration that trace-only attribution can miss the real cause. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38245v1
  - C-0055, Anthropic's own primary-source account of its cybersecurity incidents (directly read): Anthropic publicly revised its own attribution call, from "harness/operational failure" to "model alignment failure (biased reasoning + recklessness)," as more evidence arrived — a real-world case study that attribution is revisable, not a one-time verdict. https://www.anthropic.com/research/alignment-assessment-cybersecurity-incidents
  - C-0062, TestSuiteBench (abstract read directly): frontier coding agents generate weak test suites from specifications (mutation scores under 60%) despite the suites looking valid — "agents can build software they cannot verify," directly relevant to LayerLens's own benchmark-generation flows. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13065
  - C-0067, MASCope (abstract read directly): recovers communication topology from multi-agent traces without explicit labels and uses it to diagnose coordination failures (chi^2=409.9 topology-failure association) at ~6% the cost of repeated LLM diagnosis — a concrete method for attribution *within* a multi-agent system. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10126
  - C-0068, "Monitor-Validity Failures" (abstract read directly, 2026-10-26): 18 confirmed cases where a safety monitor correctly recognized policy-relevant evidence but the framework's own plumbing stranded it before it reached the enforcement decision — a new attribution category one level more specific than "the monitor is wrong." https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23362v1
  - C-0072, "Between Spoilers and Guesswork" (abstract read directly, 2026-10-26): several widely-used coding benchmarks leak ground truth into the task statement itself while trying to fix under-specification — a self-inflicted V2-style vulnerability baked into the task, distinct from BenchJack's runtime-accessible-ground-truth framing. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22760v1
  - C-0076, Diviner (abstract read directly, 2026-10-26): a real production agentic RCA system at Meta operationalizes a four-stage attribution lifecycle, scoring investigations against responder-reviewed ground truth for exact cause, useful direction, and "Groundedness" — the most concrete real-world precedent yet for operationalizing attribution evaluation. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21281v1
  - C-0079, Annotrace (abstract read directly, 2026-10-26): a cross-format trace-annotation workbench standardizing "a step" (not a raw log line) as the unit of attribution across six trace formats, reaching 91% human-AI annotator agreement. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23284v1
  - C-0094, CRM-Agent-Bench (abstract read directly, 2026-11-02): a Salesforce-adjacent benchmark independently re-derives a four-facet "outcome is not enough" grading scheme (outcome, trajectory, operational viability, policy compliance) for stateful CRM tasks, the closest external match yet to LayerLens's own answer/actions/judge/state grader taxonomy. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32004
  - C-0096, Large-Scale Study of GitHub Copilot Trajectories (abstract read directly, 2026-11-02): 7,581 real Copilot PRs find 58% see no human intervention at all, and interventions are brief (median 2 loops) and front-loaded — a real calibration point for what an attribution/review surface needs to support (an infrequent, high-leverage interrupt). https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32313
  - C-0097, "Who Verifies the Tools? How Warped Formatters Mislead Agents and Their Verifiers" (abstract read directly, 2026-11-02): a real production incident where tool-output formatting (not adversarial gaming) misled both an agent and its verifiers, flipping verifier acceptance 90/90 wrong to 90/90 correct from a wording fix alone — a misleading tool-output *format* is its own nameable attribution category, distinct from a misleading error message. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32110
  - C-0103, C-0104, Anthropic's fifth cybersecurity incident (UK AISI, directly read, 2026-11-02): a further case study of attribution being causally tested, not just asserted — editing reasoning passages to surface possible harm cut an attack rate from 83% to 13%, and Anthropic explicitly separated task misconfiguration (environment) from "a serious failure of alignment" (model). https://www.anthropic.com/research/alignment-assessment-ukaisi
  - C-0108, Patronus's TRAIL benchmark (incomplete access, 2026-11-02): a dedicated external benchmark for agentic trace-error localization finds SOTA models score under 12% at jointly locating and typing errors — a useful yardstick for how far ahead of the field LayerLens's own backend attribution truth already is. https://www.patronus.ai/blog/introducing-trail-a-benchmark-for-agentic-evaluation
  - C-0114, EnvOS (abstract read directly, 2026-11-09): explicitly separates, before any agent runs, whether a future failure would trace to the agent, a scripted change that didn't fire, an update the agent never received, or a grader that scored wrongly — the cleanest unification yet of feasibility and attribution into one certification pipeline. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40246v1
  - C-0112, Tracekit Box (abstract read directly, 2026-11-09): an open, fail-closed micro-VM reference design where every egress path is a signed, host-gated door, ending in an offline-verifiable `.tkb` bundle — the closest open design yet to the trust wall's own "the agent cannot see behind this boundary" shape. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38458v1
  - C-0113, Ring-0 by Default / AaOS (abstract read directly, 2026-11-09): a systematic 116-mechanism/19-subsystem map finds deployed agent harnesses (Claude Code, Codex CLI, Gemini CLI) miss 65 OS-grade protections outright, including tamper-proof audit; all three CLIs were found to load forged transcript records on resume — a ready-made checklist for auditing any harness-adjacent surface LayerLens's own system types sit behind. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.33753v1
  - C-0116, Soei (abstract read directly, 2026-11-09): a 45-session, 102,233-record real production study finds 57.8% of to-dos marked complete after editing had no proof in between — large-scale confirmation that an agent's own completion claim should never stand in as evidence. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39468v1
  - C-0126, Arize's own production incident (incomplete access, 2026-11-09): a web page talked a support agent into leaking a different customer's data on 28 of 100 runs, caught via an Arize Phoenix trace — a concrete, quantified external calibration point for a real indirect-prompt-injection attribution case. https://arize.com/blog/indirect-prompt-injection-ai-agents/
- **Hypothesis**: surface attribution as a location on the trace (where it broke) with a routed next action, not a log line, and make the trust wall's guarantees visible so a passing score is legibly not gameable.
- **How to validate**: product check on the current attribution UI (open Q#2); design the trace-location surface; test whether users correctly assign blame faster.
- **Maturity**: L1 developing — now twenty-five independent corroborating sources plus the core differentiator, including one (C-0022) that validates a choice already shipped in V1, one (C-0039) whose own approach (prospective instrumentation) is the closest external analogue yet to LayerLens's trust wall, two (C-0042, C-0043) grounded in real production/enterprise deployments, a directly-read real-world case study of attribution being revised in public (C-0055, extended this run by C-0103/C-0104), a concrete multi-agent attribution method (C-0067), a real production RCA system (C-0076) plus concrete trace-annotation tooling (C-0079), a close product-shape match (C-0094), a real-world calibration on how often humans actually intervene (C-0096), a real production formatting-defect incident (C-0097), an external field-wide yardstick (C-0108), and (new this run) EnvOS's unified feasibility/attribution certification pipeline, an open reference isolation design and a systematic harness-protection audit (both directly relevant to making the trust wall's own guarantees visible), a large-scale real-production confirmation that completion claims are not evidence, and a second real production indirect-injection incident. To reach L2: the product-check result and a named user outcome measured against today's UI.

## I-0004: Comparability guardrails and always show a tradeoff axis

- **Problem / pain point**: comparing runs or models on one accuracy number hides version mismatches and cost/reliability tradeoffs. A 1-point accuracy edge can hide a 10x cost difference.
- **Persona**: Taylor (accountable lead), Alex (analyst), Evan.
- **User outcome**: users compare only comparable runs, and never rank on accuracy alone.
- **Open design question(s)**: #1 (comparability), #9 (comparison as the signature surface).
- **Evidence**:
  - C-0003, tau2-bench v1.0.1: maintainers had to declare scores non-comparable across a grading-fix version. https://github.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/releases
  - C-0009, HAL (Princeton): cost-aware Pareto-frontier comparison, archived to pursue reliability. https://github.com/princeton-pli/hal-harness
  - C-0044, "Finding the Right Fit" (directly read via companion repo): model rankings reverse across agent harnesses with hard numbers (Claude beats GPT by 7.9 points under one harness, trails by 30.1 under another, same benchmark); a model's own vendor harness is not reliably its best, and higher cost does not reliably buy a higher score. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00917v1
  - C-0066, "A Baseline Assessment for the Agentic Web Technology Stack" (abstract read directly): the full protocol+framework+model stack, not just harness alone, is a confound for task completion rate, efficiency, and verbosity. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13703
  - C-0058, "Same Query, Different Answer" (abstract read directly): re-executing published benchmarks at scale (1.2M model calls) finds 21% of a benchmark's own already-published results change quality on re-execution — a comparability caution at the level of "did this score even replicate," prior to any cross-run comparison. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13823
  - C-0071, GatewayBench (abstract read directly, 2026-10-26): MCP gateway design is its own comparability confound, interacting strongly with the underlying model and agent harness — a further dimension beyond harness/scaffold/protocol. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23449v1
  - C-0073, ToolBottleneckBench (abstract read directly, 2026-10-26): harness completion-cap budget and tool-menu visibility, not model reasoning, drive most tool-agent failures and can look like a capability gap; raising the cap sixteenfold removed most of the penalty. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21098v1
  - C-0093, "When Agents Fail: Model or Harness--Who's to Blame?" (abstract read directly, 2026-11-02): the largest-scale confirmation yet (111 settings, 52,168 trajectories) that harness rankings vary across models and benchmarks, and similar task scores can accompany distinct execution profiles — independently corroborates and extends C-0044. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32483
  - C-0094, CRM-Agent-Bench (abstract read directly, 2026-11-02): across sixteen evaluated systems, closely-grouped leading scorers (64.32%-83.60% strict success) diverged sharply in cost and per-domain reliability — another concrete "don't rank on the top-line number alone" data point. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32004
  - C-0106, HarnessMM (abstract read directly, 2026-11-02): guarded decision reuse cuts harness token cost up to 87.93% without hurting (and in one pairing, improving) task success — cost is a moving target even holding model and harness identity fixed. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32813
  - C-0115, TetraState (abstract read directly, 2026-11-09): on a 506-test benchmark, two harness revisions moved modal accuracy by only 0.2 and 2.6 points yet changed 11 and 214 individual test labels — near-identical top-line scores can mask large per-test churn, a concrete worked example of exactly the comparison-view failure mode this idea's hypothesis needs to guard against. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41130v1
  - C-0125, Anthropic's own Managed Agents post (directly read, 2026-11-09): Anthropic's newest hosted-agent infrastructure states harnesses "can go stale as models improve" but is itself silent on versioning, reproducibility, or comparability — evidence that open Q#1/Q#5 remain unclaimed white space industry-wide, not a gap specific to LayerLens. https://www.anthropic.com/research/managed-agents
- **Hypothesis**: LayerLens's immutable versions already block averaging across mismatches; extend that to always show at least one tradeoff axis (cost or reliability) next to the primary score, HAL-style, rather than a single winner.
- **Build caution (new, 2026-10-12)**: C-0044 shows harness/scaffold is as large a confound as model choice or cost — any comparison view should pin and surface harness identity as a first-class dimension, not treat "model" as the whole story.
- **Build caution (new, 2026-10-19)**: C-0066 extends this to the full technology stack (protocol + framework), and C-0058 is a reminder that even a single, un-compared score may not survive its own re-execution — comparability starts before comparison.
- **Build caution (new, 2026-10-26)**: C-0071/C-0073 extend the confound list to MCP gateway configuration and harness *budget* parameters (completion caps, visible tool-menu size) specifically — a comparison view should pin these, not only harness/model identity.
- **Build caution (new, 2026-11-02)**: C-0106 adds harness-internal cost-optimization engineering (decision reuse) as yet another reason two runs with the same model and harness *name* may not be truly comparable on cost — a comparison view may need a harness *version* or configuration fingerprint, not just a name.
- **Build caution (new, 2026-11-09)**: TetraState (C-0115) is a concrete worked example, not just a caution — a comparison view that only watches the aggregate/modal score would have missed a 214-test label flip entirely; Anthropic's own newest agent infrastructure (C-0125) confirms this white space is still unclaimed even by a frontier lab's own hosted product.
- **How to validate**: product check, do comparison views show cost/reliability by default today (open Q#1); prototype a Pareto-style comparison.
- **Maturity**: L1 developing (twelve sources, at the largest scale yet recorded for the harness-confound sub-cluster). To reach L2: the product-check result and a validation plan for the tradeoff view.
- **Last update**: 2026-11-09.

## I-0005: Flag same-family judge/agent pairings

- **Problem / pain point**: an AI judge quietly favors its own model family, biasing verdicts, with no visible signal.
- **Persona**: Evan, Alex, Riley.
- **User outcome**: judge verdicts trusted appropriately; bias surfaced, not hidden.
- **Open design question(s)**: #2 (attribution), differentiator #3 (reproducible, defensible grading).
- **Evidence** (accumulating):
  - C-0002, "The Judge in the Mirror" (pilot): mean self-preference index +0.14, present even without self-recognition. https://github.com/hankimis/self-preference
  - C-0014, "LLMs Trust Their Own" (12 open-weight models, 9 tasks): in-group consensus increases conformity, out-group decreases it, independent of correctness. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33495
  - Backed by thesis T-05 (structural identity-linked judge bias).
- **Hypothesis**: when a Judge model shares a vendor/family with the agent under test, flag it and suggest a cross-family judge or a deterministic Grader.
- **How to validate**: cross-family re-judge test (2026-W39 brief), then a product check on whether the Judge/agent model identities are even surfaced today.
- **Maturity**: **L1 developing (upgraded 2026-09-29** from L0): two independent evidence families now, plus a promoted thesis. To reach L2: the product check result plus a named user outcome.
- **Last update**: 2026-09-29.

## I-0007: Make environment lifecycle legible, and let users checkpoint/branch a run

- **Problem / pain point**: users cannot see, while working in an environment, what state it keeps across a run, when it will stop existing, or cheaply save a checkpoint and branch off to try a variation. Lifecycle is invisible.
- **Persona**: Devon (agent engineer), Evan (evaluation owner).
- **User outcome**: users understand and control an environment's state and lifetime, and can explore variants (reproduce, branch, compare) without rebuilding, which also strengthens reproducibility.
- **Open design question(s)**: env state/lifetime (control and recovery) and deterministic replay as a visible surface.
- **Evidence**:
  - C-0012, DeepSeek DSec: stateful pause/resume via snapshots and `pack_diff` (checkpoint a sandbox, restore as a new one) exist as backend capabilities at industrial scale. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22978

## I-0008: Establish ground truth for a user-built or prod-imported world

- **Problem / pain point**: the stated goal is a synthetic world users can fully mess with AND that mirrors their real prod, so they can thoroughly test agents. But a world a user hand-edits or imports from prod is realistic yet arrives with no answer key, so "what is correct" is unknown and agents cannot be graded against it deterministically.
- **Persona**: Devon (agent engineer), Evan (evaluation owner).
- **User outcome**: users can bring in (or freely build) their real world and still get trustworthy, gradeable results, keeping realism and verifiability together instead of trading one for the other.
- **Open design question(s)**: task feasibility and synthetic-data legibility (where ground truth comes from and how it is validated).
- **Framing correction (from the 2026-09-29 discussion)**: it is NOT realism vs verifiability. Mutation, branching, and checkpointing are all realistic and verifiable, because grading looks at outcomes (actions/state graders already do this), and a snapshot is just a reproducible starting point. The real constraint is narrow: any state you want to GRADE against must be reproducible and have a knowable correct answer. Minted worlds get the answer key for free; user-built or prod-imported worlds do not, so this idea is about capturing ground truth for the realistic end of that spectrum, and guiding which grader (answer / actions / state / judge) applies where.
- **Evidence**:
  - C-0012, DeepSeek DSec: prompted the statefulness/mutability discussion (stateful, branchable worlds at scale). https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22978
  - C-0091, UserArena (abstract read directly, 2026-11-02): a real-world e-commerce benchmark (64,729 trajectories) supports offline validation of a simulator against outcomes from a controlled production A/B test, with the authors' own method "most closely reproducing the production policy effect in offline simulation" — the first external research evidence for exactly the validation method this idea's own "how to validate" section calls for. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30932
  - Internal: builds on LayerLens's existing learned-world (connect-source) capability and the actions/state/judge grader types.
- **Hypothesis**: if we give users a way to declare or infer what "correct" means for a world we did not mint, we extend verifiable grading from minted worlds to imported/edited ones, unlocking "bring your real world and still trust the verdict."
- **How to validate**: external corroboration or a direct user-need signal (an actual user asking to grade against their own imported world), plus a prototype of a ground-truth-capture flow.
- **Maturity**: **L1 developing (promoted 2026-11-02** from L0): UserArena is the second source this idea needed — a published, external methodology for validating a simulator/world against real production outcomes, satisfying this idea's own stated L1 bar. Still the thinnest L1 idea in the backlog: no real LayerLens user-need signal yet, and UserArena's domain (e-commerce recommendation) is adjacent, not identical, to LayerLens's own system types.
- **Last update**: 2026-11-02.

## I-0009: Agent-actionable error and recovery guidance in tool/environment surfaces

- **Problem / pain point**: Tool and API error messages are written for human developers (run a command, edit a config, open a web page). Agents that read these messages often follow the human-oriented step literally, and fail to recover — and, counterintuitively, more capable models follow the bad step more faithfully, not less.
- **Persona**: Devon (agent engineer, debugging why an agent got stuck on a real system); Evan (evaluation owner, wants failures attributed to the right cause).
- **User outcome**: agents recover from real, tool-level failures inside a LayerLens environment instead of silently following advice they cannot act on; failures get attributed to "misleading tool surface" rather than misfiled as "agent error."
- **Open design question(s)**: #2 (failure attribution — a misleading error message is its own nameable failure class), #7 (recovery).
- **Evidence**:
  - C-0018, "MCP Error Messages Written for Developers Hurt the Most Capable Agents Most": in 150 MCP servers, 949/3,001 error messages give human-oriented next steps; following them left recovery as low as 6% (rate limits) or cost up to 69 points of task score (credentials, GPT-6 Astra); naming an actual tool call in the message raised recovery to 84-88%. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35381
  - Connects to new thesis T-07 (capability amplifies, not dampens, exploitation of flawed interfaces) via C-0021.
  - C-0078, "Robust Function Calling for LLM Agents Under Interface Drift" (abstract read directly, 2026-10-26): a second independent source and mechanism — post-deployment "interface drift" (changed tool descriptions/schemas/candidate lists) breaks function calling even with fixed tool semantics; a training-free repair layer (SchemaShield-Lite) recovered 19/22 and 33/45 drift-induced failures across two benchmarks. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23342v1
  - C-0086, Arize's Harbor+Phoenix tool-description benchmark (incomplete access, 2026-10-26): a concrete, quantified example from the other direction — a single tool-description edit cut an agent's unnecessary skill loads from 51 to zero and lowered benchmark cost 30%, correctness unchanged. https://arize.com/blog/how-to-test-agent-tool-descriptions-with-harbor-and-phoenix/
  - C-0081, Android-agent source-authorization (abstract read directly, 2026-10-26): a related but distinct angle in a new domain — an agent cannot verify the *provenance* of content it observes before acting, a different gap from misleading error messages or schema drift, but the same family of "the tool/interface surface itself, not the model's reasoning, is where the gap is." https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15502v1
  - C-0095, "Tool-Calling LLM Agents Under Realistic MCP Faults" (abstract read directly, 2026-11-02): a controlled, 1,350-cell fault-injection experiment calibrated to the real public MCP ecosystem (57,240 issue/PR threads, 1,774 repos) finds a larger model retains no larger share of its fault-free score under faults than a smaller one (p=0.96) — "scale does not purchase fault tolerance," and "framework-level error handling matters more than model scale," the first genuinely controlled (not observational) evidence for this idea's whole premise. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32555
  - C-0097, "Who Verifies the Tools? How Warped Formatters Mislead Agents and Their Verifiers" (abstract read directly, 2026-11-02): a real production RAG system's tool-output formatters omitted key context in their printed text, misleading both the agent and its verifiers; in one test, verifiers flipped from accepting incorrect answers 90/90 times to rejecting them 90/90 times from a formatter-wording fix alone — a real deployed incident, not a constructed study, plus an actual remediation (merged for one of two tested open-source systems). https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32110
  - C-0101, "Knowing When to Stop: The Evidence-Action Gap in Coding Agents" (abstract read directly, 2026-11-02): extends the idea's scope from "misleading recovery guidance" to "knowing when to stop and hand off" — across 19,371 trajectories, a third of agents needing outside help still exhaust their budget despite having collected sufficient evidence; an escalation tool cuts this from 34.0% to 6.7%, but even a fired escalation ticket often omits the agent's own progress/verification status, a concrete handoff-artifact design gap. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31895
  - C-0109, Arize's confidence-gated tool-selection routing (incomplete access, 2026-11-02): a product-shaped instance of the fix this idea calls for — a DECISION span recording every rejected tool option's probability, and a confidence threshold deciding whether the agent's own pick or a dedicated decision model's pick is used, directly addressing "surface a legible near-miss record" rather than only fixing error text. https://arize.com/blog/ai-agent-tool-selection-decision-tracing/
- **Hypothesis**: LayerLens's own shipped system types (Salesforce, Linear, SEC EDGAR, Stripe, Gmail) wrap real APIs with real, human-authored error surfaces — this is not a hypothetical failure mode for us. An environment could audit or annotate its tool error surfaces for agent-actionability (does the message name a call the agent can actually make?), and surface a "misleading tool surface" attribution category distinct from agent error.
- **Dedup**: checked against I-0002 (task feasibility, about design-time solvability) and I-0006 (pre-action confirmation consequences); distinct — this is about run-time recovery guidance after a tool failure, not before-the-fact confirmation or task-level solvability.
- **How to validate**: audit a sample of LayerLens's own system-type error surfaces (Salesforce/Linear/Stripe/etc.) for the same human-oriented-step pattern the paper documents; if found, prototype a tool-call-naming rewrite and measure recovery.
- **Maturity**: **L2 ready to spec (promoted 2026-11-02** from L1): now rests on six independent mechanisms across a controlled experiment (C-0095), a real production incident with an actual fix (C-0097), a large-N escalation study (C-0101), and a shipped product pattern (C-0109), in addition to the original misleading-error-message (C-0018) and interface-drift (C-0078) families — satisfying the L2 bar (multiple independent sources, a named persona, a stated user outcome, an open design question, and the concrete validation plan already written above). ## I-0011: Detect and surface stale environment observations mid-task

- **Problem / pain point**: an agent grounds its reasoning in a tool observation (e.g. a file's contents, a record's state) whose underlying source can change later in the same task — through a concurrent write, a scheduled mutation, a branch/fork, or another agent's action — while the model retains only the stale content in its context. Existing agent runtimes provide little support for signaling this, so the agent (and a human reviewing the trace) can act on, or trust, outdated facts about current state.
- **Persona**: Devon (agent engineer, debugging a trace where the agent's claims don't match a later system check); Evan (evaluation owner, wants a failure correctly attributed to staleness rather than agent error or environment error).
- **User outcome**: agents (and the people reviewing their traces) correctly distinguish "the agent acted on information that was true when observed, but changed since" from "the agent made an error" or "the environment behaved incorrectly" — a new, legible failure category, with less wasted debugging effort re-deriving why a trace looks inconsistent.
- **Open design question(s)**: #2 (failure attribution — staleness as its own nameable category), #7 (recovery — once staleness is detected, what should happen: re-fetch, annotate, or suppress the stale observation).
- **Evidence**:
  - C-0064, "When Agent Context Goes Stale" (abstract read directly): Concord links each tool observation to its source, detects source changes, and applies configurable policies (update/annotate/suppress) before reuse; on a purpose-built benchmark it matched oracle recovery in all evaluated cases across three frontier models while using 46.4% fewer tokens than the strongest non-oracle baseline. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.05281
  - C-0099, "When Is a Memory Safe Enough to Act On?" / AMV-Guard (abstract read directly, 2026-11-02): a second independent source and a mechanistically distinct mechanism — frames whether a memory premise must be re-checked as a property of the *pending action's consequence*, not the memory entry itself, via a value-of-information gate; on ToolSandbox, cuts mean verification cost 33.9% while keeping 100% safe-proceed recall. Where Concord asks "has the source changed," AMV-Guard asks "given what I'm about to do, is this premise worth re-checking" — two genuinely different angles on the same pain point. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31446
  - C-0107, Patronus's MEMTRACK (incomplete access, 2026-11-02): a third, adjacent angle — a dedicated benchmark (simulated Linear/Slack/Git organizational timeline) finds agents largely fail to even invoke memory tools effectively, a prerequisite problem any staleness-flagging mechanism (Concord, AMV-Guard) would sit on top of; domain is close to LayerLens's own shipped Linear system type. https://www.patronus.ai/blog/memtrack
- **Hypothesis**: LayerLens's environments are mutable and can be branched/forked/replayed (a core architectural property); any of those operations could leave a running agent holding stale observations about environment state from before the mutation. A lightweight staleness-detection layer (link each tool response to the environment-state version it was read from; flag or re-serve when that version changes mid-task) would make this failure category visible and attributable, rather than silently confusing a trace.
- **Dedup**: checked against I-0009 (agent-actionable error/recovery guidance — distinct: that is about error messages being misleading post-failure; this is about tool *observations* going stale pre-failure, with no error signal at all) and T-06/I-0007 (adjacent but distinct: T-06 is about environment *state* lifecycle from the system's side; this is about the *agent's belief* about that state going stale, a legibility problem on the agent's side of the same boundary). Distinct because no existing idea addresses observation invalidation specifically.
- **How to validate**: a product check on whether any LayerLens environment mutation (a branch, a concurrent edit, a scheduled change) can occur mid-task today, and if so, whether a running agent's prior tool observations are ever invalidated or re-served; a design sketch of a staleness flag in the trace UI.
- **Maturity**: **L1 developing (promoted 2026-11-02** from L0): a second independent source and mechanism (C-0099, action-conditioned verification) now exists alongside C-0064 (source-change detection), satisfying the maturity ladder's "multiple independent sources" bar; a third, adjacent source (C-0107) grounds the pain point in a real benchmark close to LayerLens's own Linear system type. To reach L2: a named user outcome plus a validation plan, ideally confirmed with a product check.
- **Last update**: 2026-11-02.

## I-0012: Detect cross-document information-aggregation leaks in mixed-clearance environments

- **Problem / pain point**: An agent can retrieve from several individually-permitted documents or records and assemble a sensitive conclusion none of them disclosed alone (a "mosaic effect"). This is distinct from a credential/secret leaking (the trust-wall territory) — it's the agent's own legitimate, permitted access to multiple records combining into a disclosure nobody individually authorized.
- **Persona**: Devon (agent engineer, building an environment whose system types carry mixed-sensitivity records), Riley (reviewer/compliance, who would need to know this risk class exists before accepting a claim of "safe").
- **User outcome**: an environment author can see, and a reviewer can verify, that an agent's answer did not assemble a disclosure from several individually-fine records that none of them individually authorized.
- **Open design question(s)**: general (no existing numbered open question covers this precisely; closest is #2, attribution, since a mosaic-leak would be its own nameable failure category distinct from agent error or environment error).
- **Evidence**:
  - C-0092, Agent Mosaic (abstract read directly, 2026-11-02): across 30 synthetic cybersecurity/medical/military scenarios (10,800 simulated answers, 1,440 live-harness answers), the best model (GPT-5.6-Sol) disclosed forbidden composites in 7.08% of simulated answers (worse live), even under benign, single-document-answerable queries; visible classification headers plus explicit aggregation rules reduced but did not eliminate disclosure (32.37% still leaked); refusals did not reliably protect confidentiality either. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30418
- **Hypothesis**: LayerLens mints environments populated with real or synthetic records across its own system types (Salesforce, Gmail, SEC EDGAR, Stripe, Linear) that plausibly carry mixed sensitivity even within one tenant. An agent operating across several of an environment's own records could, in principle, assemble a disclosure neither document alone would authorize. An environment could flag or test for this via a dedicated grader checking whether an agent's final answer combines information from records whose combination was never individually authorized.
- **Dedup**: checked against I-0009 (tool/error-surface guidance — distinct, this is about information aggregation across legitimately-accessed records, not tool-call recovery), I-0006 (consequence-naming confirmations — distinct, this is about what an agent is allowed to *conclude and say*, not a pending write action), and the general trust-wall/credential-isolation cluster under T-02 (distinct — that protects secrets/seeds from the agent; this is about the agent's own legitimate access to multiple records combining into something none of them individually discloses). Distinct enough from all existing ideas to merit a new entry.
- **How to validate**: a product check on whether any LayerLens system type's records carry mixed sensitivity within one tenant today, and if so, whether any existing grader would catch a mosaic-style disclosure; a small test constructing a scenario where two LayerLens-minted records combine into a disclosure neither authorizes alone, and checking whether any shipped grader flags it.
- **Maturity**: L0 nascent (one source, newly created this run). To reach L1: a second independent source on cross-document aggregation leakage, or a product-check result confirming (or ruling out) the failure mode in a LayerLens environment today.
- **Last update**: 2026-11-02.

## I-0013: Track provenance and authority on memory/retrieved content, not just its contents

- **Problem / pain point**: Today's trust-wall thinking (and I-0011's staleness work) protects *content* — is a secret hidden, is an observation current — but not *authorship*: nothing in a typical agent memory or retrieved-content pipeline records who actually said something or with what authority, which lets an attacker-controlled source (a crafted web page, a nested social-media post) get misattributed as the user's own instruction or preference.
- **Persona**: Devon (agent engineer, building an environment whose system types or agent-under-test carry any form of cross-call or cross-session memory), Riley (reviewer, needs to know whether a recorded "preference" or instruction can be trusted as genuinely the user's).
- **User outcome**: a memory/retrieval pipeline cannot have its content's claimed authorship silently escalated; an attacker's own words never get recorded as the user's, and any such attempt becomes a visible, structural protection fault rather than a silent trace artifact.
- **Open design question(s)**: #7 (human control — a misattributed "preference" is a silent authority escalation); general scope area "context and memory" per PROTOCOL.md (no existing numbered open question covers authorship/authority precisely).
- **Evidence**:
  - C-0123, Role Grounding Attack (abstract read directly, 2026-11-09): a formalized attack where a nested social-media post tricks a victim agent's memory into misattributing an attacker's preference to its own user, achieving high success rates across five memory systems and six models (instantiated as tool-selection manipulation and privacy leakage). https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38209v1
  - C-0124, ClawMMU (abstract read directly, 2026-11-09): the direct structural countermeasure — label every memory entry with owner, scope, origin, and authority; check these labels at every context-entry/storage/tool-call boundary; the central rule is that authority may never self-elevate, turning a memory attack into a classical, harness-level protection fault even when the model itself is fooled. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28093v2
- **Hypothesis**: if LayerLens's own agents-under-test, or any future cross-session/cross-environment memory capability, ever carry forward retrieved or remembered content, the same misattribution class (an injected source's words recorded as the user's own) is a plausible, currently-unaddressed risk — worth a design-review checklist item even before it is a shipped capability.
- **Dedup**: checked against I-0011 (stale observations — about content going *out of date*, not about *who said it*) and the existing trust-wall/credential-isolation cluster under T-02 (about secrets/seeds staying hidden from the agent, not about authorship of non-secret content). Distinct: this is specifically about authority/provenance tracking, a gap neither existing idea addresses.
- **How to validate**: a product check on whether any LayerLens system type or agent-under-test carries memory/context across more than one task/session today, and if so, whether any mechanism tracks the authority/origin of a remembered item; if a future memory capability is ever built, test it directly against the Role Grounding Attack's own instantiation (tool-selection manipulation, privacy leakage).
- **Maturity**: L1 developing (two independent sources — an attack and a structurally distinct, independently-authored defense mechanism — at creation; satisfies the L1 bar directly). To reach L2: a named LayerLens-specific user outcome and a validation plan tied to an actual or planned LayerLens memory capability.
- **Last update**: 2026-11-09 (new idea this run).

## I-0014: Make verifiable attestation visible to the reviewer, not just produced internally

- **Problem / pain point**: Open question #11 already names the gap — a risk/compliance reviewer needs to accept a claim without trusting the claimant, but LayerLens's own attestation today is API-only, with no reviewer-facing surface. - **Persona**: Riley (reviewer/compliance, the exact persona open Q#11 is framed around).
- **User outcome**: a reviewer can accept a verdict or claim (a grade, an agent's "I verified this" statement, an attestation of a run's provenance) by checking a presented proof/evidence chain themselves, rather than having to trust LayerLens's or the agent's own say-so — turning "attestation exists in the API" into "attestation is something a reviewer actually looks at and checks."

- **Evidence**:

Distinct: this is specifically about turning an internally-produced attestation into something a reviewer actively checks.
