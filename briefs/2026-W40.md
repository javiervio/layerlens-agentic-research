## One concept to understand better: "System One" decision models versus LLM-as-judge

**Plain-language explanation**: Most AI judges today are large language models asked to write a verdict in natural language ("this answer is correct because..."). A "System One" model like Jev does something narrower: instead of writing anything, it only answers questions you define in advance, from a fixed set of possible answers (yes/no, a score, a choice among options) — and it tells you how confident it is in each answer as a number. Because it isn't generating open-ended text, it can be far cheaper and faster, but it also can't explain novel reasoning or handle a question nobody defined in advance for it.

**Example**: asking a generative LLM judge "is this customer support response good?" gets you a paragraph of reasoning and a verdict. Asking Jev the same thing requires you to have already decided the specific yes/no or scored questions that add up to "good" (does it address the request? is it polite? does it contain an error?) — Jev answers exactly those, with a confidence number on each, and nothing else. The generative judge is more flexible; the typed judge is cheaper, faster, and tells you when it isn't sure.

**When it does not apply**: for open-ended, hard-to-decompose judgments (does this code correctly implement a novel algorithm? is this proof valid?) — exactly where the CMU paper found Jev falls behind — a narrow, predefined-question model doesn't have anywhere to route the judgment; you need something that can reason about a case nobody anticipated. That's also roughly where LayerLens's own Judge mechanism (rubric-tuned, versioned) already lives, distinct from a Grader's fixed answer-key check.

**Tie to LayerLens open question #2** (failure attribution): a confidence-scored verdict is itself a form of attribution — "the judge wasn't sure" becomes its own visible, nameable category, distinct from both "the judge said pass" and "the judge said fail."

**This week's exercise**: Pick one thing LayerLens's Evaluations pillar currently checks with an AI Judge or Scorer. Could that specific check be decomposed into a handful of narrow, predefined yes/no or scored questions the way Jev requires — and if so, what would you lose by doing that (what judgment calls need the flexibility of an open-ended judge)?

## Possible applications to LayerLens

**1. Problem**: LayerLens's Judge/Scorer verdicts, as far as this research knows, present a pass/fail or a 0-5 score without a visible confidence signal — so a verdict the model was barely sure about looks identical to one it was certain about. **How others solve it**: the Jev pattern (signal #1) — expose a calibrated confidence number per verdict, and route low-confidence cases to a stronger check (a different judge, a deterministic Grader, or a human) instead of showing them at face value. **The gap**: whether any confidence/probability is already computed internally by LayerLens's Judge/Scorer pipeline today but simply not surfaced is unconfirmed — this could be a near-zero-cost first step if so. **The assumption about our product**: untested; needs a product check. **A small test a designer could run**: pick one existing Judge verdict, sketch what the UI would look like with a confidence badge next to it, and what "escalate this" would mean concretely (re-run with a different Judge? flag for a human?). Ties to open question #2, feeds new idea I-0010.

**2. Problem**: as checkpoint/rollback becomes commodity infrastructure (signal #2), a "looks safe, isn't" recovery feature remains a real risk if LayerLens ever builds one over remote system-type state (Salesforce, Stripe, etc.) — restoring correctly is not the same as being permitted to resume. **How others solve it**: the newly-found Recoverability primitive proposes an explicit governance-layer decision, recorded as its own piece of evidence separate from the restore itself. **The gap**: this is a proposed design pattern, not yet a shipped or validated one — nobody has built and tested this. **The assumption about our product**: unbuilt today, so untested. **A small test a designer could run**: for one shipped remote system type, sketch what a "rule that permitted this resume" would concretely say (e.g., "no other party has touched this Salesforce record since the checkpoint") and whether that's checkable automatically or needs a human confirmation. Ties to open question #7, feeds I-0007.
