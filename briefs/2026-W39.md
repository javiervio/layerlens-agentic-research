## What deserves your attention

**1. All 8 agent benchmarks audited by BenchJack turned out to be exploitable — including SWE-bench and GAIA.** [research] A red-team scanner combining static analysis with AI-driven exploit construction audited SWE-bench Verified, SWE-bench Pro, Terminal-Bench, WebArena, FieldWorkArena, OSWorld, GAIA, and CAR-bench (4,458 tasks). All 8 were exploitable; 6 hit ~98-100% scores without solving a single task, via weak agent/evaluator isolation, accessible ground truth, RCE, or judge prompt-injection. [Source](https://github.com/benchjack/benchjack). Why it matters: this is the strongest evidence I've found that LayerLens's "trust wall" (seed/knobs never served to an agent-facing key) is closing a real, exploited gap — not a theoretical one. New this week (first run, nothing to compare against).

**2. LLM judges favor their own model family, even blind and even without recognizing their own output.** [research] A pilot study had GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o, Claude Haiku 4.5, and Claude Sonnet 4.6 blindly judge each other's answers (1,152 pairwise judgments). Every judge scored its own family above a neutral consensus baseline (mean +0.14); only Claude Sonnet 4.6 could reliably tell which answer was its own, yet the bias showed up in models that couldn't. [Source](https://github.com/hankimis/self-preference). Why it matters: LayerLens already separates deterministic Graders from rubric-based Judges — this is quantified support for keeping that separation, and a reason to consider flagging same-family judge/agent pairings in the UI. New this week; pilot scale, treat as a lead to corroborate, not settled.

**3. tau2-bench had to tell its own users their scores don't compare across a patch version.** [competitive] Sierra Research's release notes for tau2-bench v1.0.1 state plainly that a grading-bug fix means results from before and after that version aren't comparable — and, usefully, they reported which direction and how much scores moved (up only, up to ~9 points) rather than just saying "don't compare." [Source](https://github.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench/releases). Why it matters: this is a real, dated, named example of exactly the problem LayerLens's immutable-versioning ("On record") differentiator exists to prevent — worth using as the go-to external example when explaining that pillar to someone non-technical. New this week (release dated 2026-07-22).

## One concept to understand better: Self-preference bias in LLM-as-judge

**Plain-language explanation**: When you ask a model to judge two answers and one of them happens to be written by a model in the same family (say, both Claude), the judge tends to rate that answer a bit higher — not necessarily because it's actually better, but because of something about how the judge "recognizes" or resonates with its own family's style. The surprising part: this bias showed up even in models that, when asked directly, couldn't reliably tell which answer was theirs. The bias doesn't require self-recognition to operate.

**Example**: In the pilot study above, GPT-4o rated its own family's answers 0.21 higher (on the study's index) than a neutral panel would have. Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Claude Haiku 4.5 both showed +0.14.

**When it does not apply**: This was measured on open-ended prompts with no objective ground truth (the kind where "better" is genuinely a judgment call). It has not been shown (in what I read) to hold the same way on verifiable, ground-truth tasks — which is exactly the kind of task LayerLens's deterministic Graders are built for. So this concept is a caution about the Judges/Scorers grader types specifically, not about Graders.

**Tie to LayerLens open question**: #2 (Failure attribution in the UI) — if a Judge verdict is quietly biased by model-family, does the product's attribution logic know that, or does it treat every Judge verdict as equally neutral?

## Possible applications to LayerLens

**1. Problem**: Agent benchmarks are exploitable in structural, repeatable ways (BenchJack's V1-V8 taxonomy) — but auditing them today is a reactive, after-the-fact activity, not something built into day-one design. **How others solve it**: run a red-team scanner against an existing benchmark and report findings. **The gap**: this only tells you a benchmark *was* exploitable when audited, not that a *new* environment is exploit-resistant by construction. **The assumption about our product**: LayerLens's trust wall is assumed to structurally close V1 (no isolation) and V2 (ground truth accessible), but this hasn't been checked against BenchJack's actual taxonomy. **A small test a designer could run**: walk through BenchJack's 8 vulnerability classes one at a time against a real LayerLens environment and task, and note for each one whether it's structurally impossible, merely discouraged, or untested. Ties to open question #2.

**2. Problem**: A Judge's verdict can be quietly skewed by model-family self-preference, with no visible signal that anything is wrong. **How others solve it**: nobody I found has a shipped mitigation — the field is still at the "measuring it" stage. **The gap**: there's no product-side warning when a Judge and the agent-under-test share a vendor/family. **The assumption about our product**: the three-grader-type split (Graders/Judges/Scorers) is assumed to be enough of a safeguard because Graders stay load-bearing — but that only helps if a team actually knows to keep the Judge's verdict as advisory rather than final. **A small test a designer could run**: take one existing eval where the Judge and agent share a family, re-run the identical task/output pair through a cross-family judge, and see whether verdicts diverge enough to matter. Ties to open question #2 and differentiator #3.
